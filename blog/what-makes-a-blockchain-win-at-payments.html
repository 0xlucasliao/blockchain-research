What makes a blockchain
win at payments?

Written 29 April 2026 · Last updated 29 April 2026

Payments infrastructure is how chains signal seriousness to merchants, PSPs, and builders. This note adapts a chain payments playbook: the layered SDK surface (merchant checkout, agentic MPP, subscriptions, PSP stack, virtual cards), why narrative precedes raw volume, and what “complete” looks like next to Base, Solana, and Polygon.

Infrastructure · Agentic commerce · Virtual cards · PSP integration

01
Context

Payments Is a Perception Game — Until It Isn't

Look at the chains that have dominated the developer and enterprise narrative over the last two years. Base. Solana. Polygon. They share something in common that has nothing to do with throughput benchmarks or fee schedules: they each shipped a payments story, complete with branded SDKs, merchant tooling, and PSP integrations.

The direct on-chain volume from these tools is, in absolute terms, modest. That is not the point. The point is what the tooling signals: that the chain is a serious participant in the real economy, not a speculative sideshow. Payments infrastructure functions as a credibility proof. It tells merchants, fintechs, PSPs, and eventually regulators: we are building for commerce, not just for traders.

"The chains that built payments suites did not just gain transaction volume. They gained the right to be in the room when the enterprise procurement conversation happens."

The a16z crypto team put it well in their April 2026 market map: stablecoins are giving rise to a new form of banking-as-a-service where account, payment, FX, and credit primitives combine into end-to-end financial products. Chains are the settlement layer underneath all of it. But only the chains that make themselves legible to builders will capture that position.

This document is a blueprint. It describes the four components any chain needs to build a credible, complete payments platform — and why each one matters more than it might appear.

03
Layer 1

Merchant Payments SDK

The core merchant problem is this: a business wants to accept crypto, but their payment service provider — Stripe, Adyen, Checkout.com — does not offer it on their chain of choice. They face a choice between building something custom, or not accepting crypto at all. For most merchants, the answer is the latter.

A Merchant Payments SDK eliminates that friction. The reference model is Base Pay, which ships as an embeddable checkout button backed by a USDC payment flow, and integrates natively with Shopify's payment infrastructure (itself powered by Stripe). The result is one-click crypto checkout available to millions of merchants with zero custom integration work on their part.

"As a merchant, I want to accept crypto payments, but my PSP does not have this option." The SDK is the answer — not a PSP negotiation, not a custom integration, not a whitepaper. An SDK that works today.

What the SDK needs to ship

  • Payment button component — drop-in embed with chain-native checkout flow, verification step, and polling until confirmation
  • Unique Gwei bump — deterministic transaction identification using a fractional gas increment, so the merchant backend can match payment to order without requiring a memo field
  • Pre-declared wallet address — static merchant address mode for simple integrations
  • Rotating merchant wallet set — for high-volume merchants who need to distribute inflows and reduce address-linking risk
  • Playground UI — a no-code sandbox where a merchant can test the full payment flow before going live, with testnet tokens
  • Framework variants — React, Vue, vanilla JS, and a hosted payment page for no-code storefronts

04
Layer 2

Agentic Payments SDK (MPP)

The next layer is the one most chains have not built yet — and the one where the largest long-term surface area exists. Agentic payments.

AI agents are beginning to make purchases on behalf of users. Not hypothetically: Google has donated its Agent Payments Protocol (AP2) to the FIDO Alliance, with an explicit v0.2 spec for "Human Not Present" payments — transactions executed autonomously by an agent based on pre-authorized user instructions. Mastercard has co-developed a compatible standard called Verifiable Intent that creates a tamper-proof log of user-authorized agent actions.

The commerce infrastructure is already being designed around agents. Chains need to be compatible with it.

"As an AI agent, I want to use MCP (Model Context Protocol) to perform agentic payments on behalf of a user." The MPP SDK is the interface between the agent runtime and the payment rails.

Solana has led here with the open-source MPP SDK, which wraps the chain's payment primitives in an interface consumable by agent frameworks like LangChain, AutoGen, and CrewAI. The architecture is a protocol layer: agents describe payment intent in structured JSON, the SDK validates against user-defined constraints, signs and broadcasts the transaction, and returns a receipt the agent can act on.

What the MPP SDK enables

  • Agent-initiated payments with pre-authorized spend limits and whitelist constraints
  • MCP-compatible tool interface so any MCP-enabled agent can call chain payments as a tool
  • Human-Not-Present transaction signing compatible with the FIDO AP2 spec
  • Verifiable receipt with on-chain proof, consumable by the agent's memory layer
  • Webhooks for transaction status so agent workflows can branch on payment confirmation

05
Layer 3

Subscriptions SDK

Recurring payments are one of the most structurally underserved use cases in crypto. Every SaaS company, media publisher, and service business runs on subscription revenue. None of them have a native, reliable way to collect recurring crypto payments today.

The problem is architectural. Blockchains are pull-incompatible by default — wallets cannot be debited without the user's active signature. Subscriptions require periodic, automated debits. Bridging that gap requires either user pre-authorization of an allowance smart contract, or a push-model where the user signs a recurring intent that gets executed by a keeper.

"As a merchant, I want to enable subscription payments with crypto — monthly billing, trial periods, proration — without requiring users to sign every cycle."

SDK components

  • Allowance contract — ERC-20 approve-based subscription with per-period cap; user signs once at signup
  • Keeper network integration — decentralized execution layer that triggers billing events without relying on the merchant's server
  • Billing portal component — UI for subscribers to view, pause, and cancel their plans
  • Webhook bridge — emits Stripe-compatible events so merchants can plug into their existing billing logic
  • Trial & proration support — smart contract handles partial-period billing at first charge

The Stripe compatibility layer is critical for adoption. Most merchants already operate Stripe billing infrastructure. An SDK that emits Stripe-like events — invoice.created, subscription.updated, payment_failed — means they can integrate crypto subscriptions into their existing backend logic with minimal changes. This is not about replacing Stripe; it is about plugging into the workflow it already established.

06
Layer 4

Payment Infrastructure Stack

The first three layers are developer-facing. This one is institutional. It answers the question that PSPs, neobanks, and enterprise fintechs ask when they evaluate a chain: what does it actually take to integrate with you end-to-end?

The reference here is Polygon's Open Money Stack — a curated, pre-integrated set of infrastructure partners that together cover the full payment lifecycle. Rather than requiring a PSP to negotiate five separate vendor relationships, the chain provides a tested, compatible stack they can assemble from.

"As a PSP, I want to seamlessly integrate with this chain — handling custody, off-ramps, liquidity, and FX — without building every component from scratch."

The structure above mirrors how a16z's market map describes the stablecoin infrastructure stack: FX providers bring technical integration, regional exchanges bring local market depth, banks bring correspondent relationships. A chain's infrastructure stack is the point at which all three become accessible through a single integration surface.

The strategic value compounds over time. Each PSP that integrates becomes a distribution channel, bringing their merchant base onto the chain without any further direct marketing. This is the Polygon Open Money Stack insight: become the easiest chain to integrate, and integration becomes the product.

Layer 5

Virtual Card SDK — The Bridge to Web2 Rails

The final layer is the most forward-looking. It addresses a problem that the other four layers do not: the majority of merchants on the internet today do not accept crypto natively, and they will not do so soon. Web2 payment rails — Visa, Mastercard, card-based checkout — remain the dominant interface for consumer commerce.

For agentic commerce to work at scale, agents need a way to spend in both worlds. That means programmatic access to Visa/Mastercard.

The premise: Google is building agentic commerce infrastructure (Universal Digital Payments / AP2). Amazon has the Zinc API for programmatic order placement. Most merchants where agents will shop accept cards, not crypto. The enabling layer for agents to participate in that commerce is virtual card generation — programmable, constraint-bound cards funded from a crypto treasury.

"I want to programmatically set budget and whitelist for payment tasks for my agents — and I want my agents to be able to check out using cards at standard Web2 merchants."

User-side features

  • Set a per-card spend budget drawn from on-chain stablecoin balance
  • Set a merchant whitelist — agent can only transact at approved domains
  • Generate temporary virtual cards with defined expiry — single-use or session-bound
  • Revoke any card instantly, with on-chain proof of revocation

Merchant-side features

  • API-based checkout compatible with existing card acceptance infrastructure
  • No crypto integration required — card network handles settlement as usual
  • Optionally opt into direct stablecoin settlement for lower fees and instant finality

The reason this matters beyond payments: virtual cards are the interface through which crypto treasury balances become spendable in the real economy. A business holding USDC does not need to off-ramp to a bank account to buy SaaS subscriptions, pay contractors, or procure inventory. They issue a virtual card, set the budget, and spend. That is corporate treasury management made native to on-chain assets.

Summary

The Competitive Picture

Across these five layers, the competitive positioning becomes clear. No single competitor has shipped all of them. Base has merchant payments. Solana has MPP. Polygon has the infrastructure stack. None have shipped a compelling subscriptions primitive or a virtual card SDK with agent-native controls.

The table above is not the goal — the goal is what these capabilities unlock. A chain that ships all five layers becomes the default settlement infrastructure for any fintech that needs programmable, stablecoin-native payments with both Web3 and Web2 compatibility. That is a market measured in trillions of dollars of annual payment volume.

The window is
still open.

The stablecoin infrastructure stack is still being assembled. The chains that build payments tooling now — while the developer mindshare competition is still active — will be the ones that PSPs, enterprise fintechs, and agentic commerce platforms build on by default.

Payments is not a feature. It is a go-to-market strategy. It is a signal to every builder, merchant, and institution watching the space that your chain is serious about the real economy. The tool adoption numbers are secondary. The narrative positioning is primary. And the narrative is built by shipping.
